# I1. Score before training

**Objective:** measure how often Kev-4B, as released, agrees with Jev on 1,000 test filings. That is the score
your fine-tuned model has to beat in I2.
{: .fs-6 .fw-300 }

| | |
|---|---|
| Where | Yens, 1 GPU |
| Data | 1,000 test filings (2022 to 2026) with Jev's labels, to score against |
| Model | [Kev-4B](https://huggingface.co/jaredpalmer/kev-4b) as released |

## The filings and the three questions

Every filing you score is the Item 5.02 section of a company's Form 8-K, the section that reports a change of directors
or officers. Jev answered three questions about each one: what happens to the CEO (the **status**, the main task),
**why**, and whether the change was **announced in advance**. Kev-4B answers the same three, and its score is how often
it agrees with Jev.

[The Task and the Data]({{ '/task/' | relative_url }}) explains the filings and the three questions, with every
possible answer, and walks through [five filings]({{ '/task/' | relative_url }}#five-filings) with Jev's answers. This
page refers back to two of them.

## Where the labels come from: Jev and System One

Every label you train on is one of Jev's answers. Training Kev means teaching it to give the answers Jev gives, so it
helps to know what Jev is and what DARC asked it.

### A model that makes decisions, not text

Jev is TypeSafe's main model, and its first **System One** model. TypeSafe describes System One as "built to make fast,
structured decisions that software can use directly" ([System One](https://docs.typesafe.ai/concepts/system-one)).
You send it a document, called the **state**, along with questions that have fixed answers. Jev doesn't write a
reply. It returns a probability for every answer.

There are three kinds of question:

| Type | You give | Jev returns | Here |
|---|---|---|---|
| Choice | a set of named options | the most likely option, a probability for each option, and a confidence | status (8 options), why (8 options) |
| Noul | a yes-or-no question | the probability of yes | future |
| Score | ordered levels, lowest to highest | the expected level and a probability for each level | not used |

### What DARC sent Jev

DARC sent one request per filing. Below is the request for OSI Systems ([filing 3]({{ '/task/' | relative_url }}#five-filings)), shortened. The filing text is cut
off, and the `why` question shows only one of its eight reasons. The wording comes from `scripts/questions.py`.

```json
{
  "state": "OSI SYSTEMS INC\nForm 8-K filed 2024-05-03, Item 5.02\n\nOSI Systems, Inc. (the \"Company\") announced today that Chairman and Chief Executive Officer Deepak Chopra has informed the Company's Board of Directors of his plans to retire ...",
  "questions": {
    "status": {
      "type": "choice",
      "instructions": "What happens to the company's chief executive officer, according to this filing?",
      "criteria": {
        "the CEO is not leaving the CEO position (the filing is about someone else)": null,
        "the CEO is stepping away temporarily (medical or other leave) and is expected to return": null,
        "the CEO gives up the CEO title and becomes executive chair": null,
        "the CEO gives up the CEO title and becomes non-executive (independent) chair": null,
        "the CEO gives up the CEO title and stays only as a board member (not chair)": null,
        "the CEO gives up the CEO title but stays at the company in another executive or advisory role": null,
        "the CEO is leaving the company entirely (including death)": null,
        "an interim or acting CEO is stepping aside for a permanent one": null
      }
    },
    "why": {
      "type": "choice",
      "instructions": "If the chief executive is leaving the CEO position, why, according to this filing? Judge only from what the filing says.",
      "criteria": {"the departing CEO retired voluntarily": null}
    },
    "future": {
      "type": "noul",
      "instructions": "Does the change to the CEO position take effect at a future date (announced in advance), rather than immediately or already?"
    }
  }
}
```

Jev gave a probability for each of the eight statuses (0.93 for executive chair, 0.07 for another role and 0 for the
rest). It did the same for the eight reasons, and it gave 0.98 for "announced in advance". In the data, these numbers
are the columns `p_status_1` to `p_status_8`, `p_why_1` to `p_why_8` and `p_future`.

The probabilities for one question add up to 1. For each Choice question, Jev also reports a **confidence**. It is 0
when the probability is spread evenly over all the options, and 1 when all of it is on one option. With eight
options, confidence is (top probability − 1/8) ÷ (7/8), so OSI's 0.93 gives a confidence of 0.92. In the data, it is
the column `status_conf`.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Why a decision model and not a chat model?</summary>

You could paste each filing into a chat model such as ChatGPT or Claude and ask the same three questions. For
labeling data, a decision model like Jev has several advantages.

- Every answer is one of your options. A chat model writes text, which you then have to read and sort, and it may
  answer outside your options or not answer at all. Jev's answers go straight into a table.
- You get a probability for every option, so you can see how sure the model is and check the unsure filings by hand.
- The probabilities are meant to be calibrated. TypeSafe trains Jev so that its probabilities match how often it is
  right. Of all the answers it gives 0.8, about 80% should be right. That holds across many answers, not for any one
  answer ([TypeSafe's primer](https://docs.typesafe.ai/introduction/machine-learning-primer)).
- Kev learns from the probabilities. Kev trains on Jev's whole spread of probabilities, called the **soft label**. A
  chat model's single answer is only a hard label, and from it Kev couldn't learn that Huntington Ingalls ([filing 4]({{ '/task/' | relative_url }}#five-filings))
  is a close call.
- It is cheap to run on many filings. Jev costs $42 per billion input tokens (a token is a word or a piece of one),
  and its output is free ([TypeSafe's models](https://docs.typesafe.ai/models)). All 296,477 filings come to about
  342 million input tokens.
- Kev speaks the same language. A Kev server answers the same requests in the same format, so a fine-tuned Kev can
  replace Jev without any change to your code ([Key Ideas]({{ '/concepts/' | relative_url }})).

A chat model is still the better tool when the question is open-ended, when you need to pull text out of the document
(a name, a date, a dollar amount), or when you want an explanation.

</details>

## Run it: score Kev-4B as released

Now you take **Kev-4B exactly as it was released**, with no training from you, and measure how often it agrees with
Jev on the 1,000 test filings. One Slurm job does all the work. Slurm is the system that shares out the Yens' GPUs.
You describe a job, and Slurm runs it when a GPU is free.

1. Slurm gives the job one GPU on a Yen GPU node. It is an A40 unless you ask for an H200.
2. The job loads Kev-4B onto the GPU and starts a Kev server, a program that answers the same requests Jev did.
3. It sends the server the 1,000 test filings, 16 at a time unless you choose otherwise.
4. It scores Kev-4B's answers against Jev's, and sends the speed, the GPU memory used and the scores to W&B.

> You have time for **one** job, so choose your options before you submit it. Every participant's job reports to the
> same W&B project, which is on the board. You compare your run with everyone else's there, so you don't need to run
> every option yourself.

### What each option does

#### GPU

A GPU is the chip that does the model's arithmetic, and it has its own memory. The A40 has 48 GB of memory. The H200
has 141 GB, about three times as much, and it is a newer, faster chip. The Yens have only two H200s, shared by the
whole room, so a job that asks for one may wait in the queue before it starts. The A40 is the default because there
are many more of them. You can choose the A40 or the H200.

#### Batch size

Batch size is how many filings your job sends Kev-4B at a time. Kev-4B works on them together, as one batch. You can
choose any whole number from 1 to 64. 64 is the most Kev-4B's server puts in one batch, so asking for more changes
nothing. Each filing in the batch needs some GPU memory while Kev-4B works on it, so a bigger batch uses more memory.
The server can also run smaller batches than you asked for, when filings arrive unevenly. The board shows the average
batch size it actually ran. The log and the board call the batch size you asked for `concurrency`.

#### Number format

Kev-4B is about 4 billion numbers, and each one is stored in a fixed number of bits. bf16 uses 16 bits per number and
fp32 uses 32. fp32 keeps more digits, but each number takes twice the space, so Kev-4B's weights take about 18 GB
instead of 9 GB, and the GPU has twice as much to read for every filing. You can choose bf16 or fp32.

#### Fused kernels

A kernel is a small program the GPU runs to do one step of the model, such as one large multiplication. Between
steps, the GPU writes its results to its memory and reads them back for the next step. Kev comes with its own fused
kernels, which do several steps in one program, so the GPU goes back and forth to its memory less often. With
`FUSED=0`, the job does the same math with the standard kernels from the transformers library instead. You can turn
them on or off.

### What fits in GPU memory

A GPU's memory is separate from the node's ordinary memory (RAM). Two things have to fit in it.

- Kev-4B's weights, about 4 billion numbers. In bf16 each takes 2 bytes, about 9 GB in all. In fp32 each takes 4
  bytes, about 18 GB.
- The batch. Each filing Kev-4B is working on needs room for its intermediate results, so a bigger batch takes more
  memory.

### W&B on the board

Weights & Biases (W&B) is an experiment tracker. It is a website that records every run of a job, with the settings
the run used, what it measured while it ran, and its results. Instead of opening one log file after another, you see
all the runs side by side. Every participant's I1 job reports to one W&B project, and that project is on the board,
the big screen at the front.

For each run, W&B keeps:

| W&B calls it | What it is | For your job |
|---|---|---|
| Run | one job | named after your SUNet ID, the GPU and your options, for example `<sunet>-h200-b64-fp32-<jobid>` |
| Config | the settings the run used | the GPU, batch size (W&B calls it `concurrency`), number format and fused kernels |
| Metrics | numbers sent while it runs, drawn as charts | filings per second and GPU memory used, every 100 filings |
| Summary | the final numbers | the peak GPU memory, the average batch size the server ran (`mean_batch`), and the scores |
| System | readings W&B takes from the machine itself | how busy the GPU is, and its memory |

Because every run carries its settings, the board can line up runs that differ in one option and show what that
option does to speed, memory and scores. That is why one job each is enough. Between all the participants, every option gets run.

The board shows the project's **Board** view. At the top is a bar chart for each score, with one bar per run. Below
are charts of speed and GPU memory, and a table of every run with who ran it, its options and its scores. You can open
it on your own screen too:
[Marlowe-Hackathon/Intermediate Exercise 1](https://forge.coreweave.com/wandb/Marlowe-Hackathon/Intermediate%20Exercise%201?nw=9cdpf84wp73).

#### How your job loads the key

W&B accepts a run only from a program that has a key, which works like a password. The hackathon's key is in one
file, which every Yens account can read and only DARC can change:

```
/scratch/shared/marlowe_hackathon/wandb.env
```

The file has one line: `WANDB_API_KEY=` followed by the key. When a program starts a W&B run, W&B's Python library
looks for a variable named `WANDB_API_KEY`. So your job only has to read the file before it starts Python.
`slurm/yens/intermediate/score_kev.slurm` does this before it starts Kev-4B. At its core, the line is:

```bash
set -a; source /scratch/shared/marlowe_hackathon/wandb.env; set +a
```

`source` runs the file's one line in the job's shell, which sets `WANDB_API_KEY`. Normally a variable set that way
stays in the shell. `set -a` passes every variable set after it on to the programs the job starts, such as Python,
and `set +a` turns that off again. The job also tells W&B which team and project the run belongs to:

```bash
export WANDB_ENTITY=Marlowe-Hackathon
export WANDB_PROJECT="Intermediate Exercise 1"
```

If you write a job of your own that reports to W&B, put these lines in it before the line that starts Python. Read
the key inside the job, not in your terminal. A key set in your terminal goes to every program you start from it.
Never copy the key into your clone or print it in a log, because anyone who has it can add runs to the hackathon's
projects.

### What the scores mean

Each score compares Kev-4B's answers with Jev's. On W&B, every score's name starts with `test/`, because these are the
test filings.

| Score | What it measures |
|---|---|
| `answered` | how many of the 1,000 filings Kev-4B answered |
| `status_agree_weighted` | how often Kev-4B's status matches Jev's, with each filing weighted so the 1,000 stand for all 56,334 test filings ([Describe the test set](#describe-the-test-set) explains the weights) |
| `status_agree_on_ceo_changes` | the same, on the filings Jev calls a CEO change |
| `ceo_change_f1` | how well Kev-4B tells a CEO change from not a CEO change, taking Jev's answer as right. 1 is perfect. |
| `reason_agree_on_ceo_changes` | how often Kev-4B's reason (why) matches Jev's, on the filings Jev calls a CEO change |
| `filings_per_second` | how fast Kev-4B labeled them |

Below the table, the log breaks status agreement down by Jev's status. `<jobid>_scores.json` also holds precision,
recall and agreement on the future question.

### Describe the test set

Start Python from Kev's environment, which has pandas installed:

```python
import os

```
{: .python-repl }

`d` holds every filing Jev answered, one row per filing, and `test` holds the rows from the test years. `d.columns`
lists all the columns. These are the ones you need:

| Column | What it is |
|---|---|
| `company_name`, `date_filed`, `year` | who filed and when |
| `words`, `text_502` | the length of the Item 5.02 text, and the text |
| `ceo_change` | `True` if Jev's status is anything but "not a CEO change" |
| `status_name`, `status_conf` | Jev's status and its confidence |
| `why_name`, `p_future` | Jev's reason, and the probability the change is announced in advance |
| `jev_model`, `jev_input_tokens` | which Jev answered, and the tokens it read |
| `edgar_url` | the filing on the SEC's website |

```
ceo_change
```

The sample was drawn with extra CEO changes, and extra rare statuses, on purpose, so that each one can be measured. A
random 1,000 would hold only one or two temporary leaves. Each filing carries a `weight`, the number of test filings
it stands for. Add up the weights and you get the whole test split back, 49,592 and 6,742. When you score Kev-4B on
these 1,000 filings, `scripts/intermediate/score.py` uses the weights to estimate agreement on all 56,334.

</details>
