# I2. Fine-tune on one GPU

**Objective:** train a model for 10 minutes on one Yens GPU, getting as far through Jev's labels as you can, then see
whether it agrees with Jev more often than Kev-4B as released.
{: .fs-6 .fw-300 }

| | |
|---|---|
| Where | Yens, 1 GPU |
| Data | `train.jsonl` with Jev's soft labels, to train on; 2,000 validation filings (2019 to 2021), to choose settings; I1's 1,000 test filings, to compare with Kev-4B |
| Model | Qwen 4B ([Qwen3.5-4B-Base](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-4B-Base)), fine-tuned, against Kev-4B as released |

{: .important }
> Training gets about **10 minutes**, on purpose. On a shared cluster you rarely get to train for as long as you like:
> a reservation or a job's time limit gives you a fixed slot, and the training has to fit inside it. Here the slot is
> 10 minutes, so the job trains on as many filings as fit, 512 on an A40 or 1,920 on an H200.
> [Why only 10 minutes](#why-only-10-minutes) explains how the job plans that.

You need I1's setup done, including [Copy the data]({{ '/intermediate/i1/' | relative_url }}#copy-the-data): this job reads
`train.jsonl`, `valid.jsonl` and `valid.parquet` from `$WORK/data`. Every command below runs from your copy of the
repository on the Yens:

```bash
cd /scratch/users/$USER/marlowe-hackathon
source scripts/intermediate/yens.sh
```
{: .yens }

## What you are training

You don't train all of Kev-4B. Its backbone, Qwen 4B, stays frozen, and only two small parts on top of it learn: a
**LoRA adapter** and an **answer head**, under 1% of the model. Open these if you want the details.

<details markdown="1" id="what-kev-4b-is-made-of">
<summary>What Kev-4B is made of</summary>

Kev-4B is one large model that stays as it is, with two small trained parts on top. Only the small parts are in the
release, so they are what you download and what you train.

| Part | What it is | Size | Trained here? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Backbone | Qwen3.5-4B-Base: 32 layers (24 linear attention, Gated DeltaNet, and 8 full attention), each 2,560 numbers wide | 4.21 billion numbers, 8.4 GB in bf16 | No, frozen |
| Adapter | LoRA of rank 16 on 12 kinds of matrix in every layer | 32.5 million numbers, 130 MB | Yes |
| Answer head | two small projections that score each answer option | 1.3 million numbers, 5 MB | Yes |
| Temperature | one number that softens the probabilities: 2.41 | 1 | No |

Kev never writes text. It reads the filing, then each question, laid out as the instructions, the options and a final
`<decide>` marker:

```
<state> ADVANCED MICRO DEVICES INC  Form 8-K filed 2023-01-11, Item 5.02 ...
<q> What happens to the company's chief executive officer, according to this filing?
<opt> the CEO is not leaving the CEO position (the filing is about someone else) </opt>
<opt> the CEO is stepping away temporarily (medical or other leave) and is expected to return </opt>
... six more options ...
<decide>
```
{: .output }

The answer head turns the model's state at `<decide>` into a list of 256 numbers, called the query, and its state at
each `</opt>` into another list, that option's key. Each option's score is how closely its key matches the query. A
softmax then turns the scores into probabilities that add up to 1. Dividing the scores by the temperature first makes
the probabilities less extreme. Kev-4B's temperature, 2.41, was fitted after training so that its confidence matches
how often it is right.

</details>

<details markdown="1" id="what-lora-is">
<summary>What LoRA is</summary>

A model like Qwen 4B is billions of numbers, stored in large grids called **matrices**. Each layer of the model
multiplies what comes in by its matrices. Normal fine-tuning adjusts every number in all of them.

**LoRA** (low-rank adaptation) leaves those big matrices exactly as they are, frozen, and trains a small add-on next
to each one instead. The add-on is two thin matrices, `B` and `A`. Multiplied together, they make a matrix the same
size as the big one, and that product is added to it as a correction. Because `B` and `A` are thin, they hold far
fewer numbers. For Kev-4B they are 0.8% of the model.

This has three consequences.

- Training starts exactly where the model left off. A new `B` starts at zero, so `B · A` adds nothing until training
  moves it. Continuing Kev-4B's adapter starts from its trained `A` and `B`.
- Serving costs nothing extra. When `kev.serve` loads an adapter, it adds `(α / r) · B · A` into `W` once, so a
  fine-tuned Kev-4B labels as fast as the one you timed in I1.
- It needs little memory. The optimizer, AdamW, keeps two running averages for every number it trains. For the 33.8
  million numbers of the adapter and head, the numbers, their gradients and the averages take 0.54 GB. For all 4.21
  billion numbers they would take 67 GB, more than an A40 holds.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>The math</summary>

Each layer multiplies what comes in, a list of numbers `x`, by a weight matrix `W`. LoRA adds the product of `B` and
`A` to it:

```
y = W x + (α / r) · B (A x)
```
{: .output }

`A` turns the input into `r` numbers, and `B` turns those `r` numbers into a full-size output. `r` is the **rank**, 16
for Kev-4B. `α` is a fixed scale, 32 for Kev-4B, so the correction is doubled.

An example. The MLP's `gate_proj` takes 2,560 numbers in and gives 9,216 out, so its `W` holds 2,560 × 9,216 =
23,592,960 numbers. Its `A` and `B` hold 16 × (2,560 + 9,216) = 188,416, which is 0.8% of `W`. The same holds across
the 12 kinds of matrix in all 32 layers, 32.5 million numbers in all, 0.8% of the backbone.

A rank-4 adapter has 8.1 million numbers to learn with, rank 16 has 32.5 million and rank 64 has 130 million. Kev-4B's
adapter is rank 16, and continuing it keeps rank 16. The trainer refuses any other rank, because Kev-4B's `A` and `B`
have 16 rows and columns. To try another rank, you train a new adapter from the base model.

</details>

</details>

## How fine-tuning works

Fine-tuning takes a model that already works and trains it further on your own labels, here Jev's answers. Training
repeats one **step** until it has used all the filings you give it:

1. The job takes the next filings from `train.jsonl`, 8 per step by default.
2. The **forward pass** runs each filing and its questions through the model and gives a probability for each option.
3. The **loss** compares those probabilities with Jev's. It is the cross-entropy, −Σ Jev's probability × log Kev's
   probability, and it is lowest when Kev's probabilities match Jev's, including where Jev was unsure.
4. The **backward pass** works out how each trained number should change to lower the loss. These changes, the
   **gradients**, flow back through the frozen backbone, which stays unchanged, into `A` and `B` in every layer and
   into the answer head.
5. **Clipping** scales the gradients down if their overall size is above 1.0, so that one unusual filing can't throw
   the adapter off.
6. In the **update**, AdamW moves each trained number by a small amount, set by the **learning rate**.

### Steps, batch size and learning rate

These are the words you will see in the settings and in the log:

| Term | What it means | In this job |
|---|---|---|
| Step | one round of forward pass, loss, backward pass and update | 64 steps on an A40 |
| Batch size (records per step) | how many filings' gradients are added up before one update | 8 |
| Gradient accumulation | how a big batch fits in little memory: the GPU runs one filing at a time and adds up the gradients, then updates once | `--batch 1 --accum 8` |
| Epoch | one full pass over the training file | 52,757 filings, or 6,595 steps of 8. One run here is 1% of an epoch |
| Learning rate | how far each update moves the trained numbers | 2e-5, or 2e-4 for a new adapter |
| Warmup and decay | the learning rate climbs over the first 10% of steps from a twenty-fifth of its full value, then falls along a curve to almost zero at the last step | why the job fixes the number of steps before it starts |
| Loss | how far Kev's probabilities are from Jev's: lower is better | printed every 10 steps |
| Gradient clipping | scales the gradients down when their overall size is above 1.0, so one unusual filing can't throw training off | on |
| Mixed precision | arithmetic in bf16, which the GPU runs fast, while the trained numbers stay in fp32 | on |
| Gradient checkpointing | keeps only some of the intermediate results the backward pass needs and recomputes the rest, trading time for memory | on |

More filings per step give smoother updates, but fewer of them from the same filings. The learning rate and the batch
size are the two settings that matter most, and they work together.

The model learns only from the training set. Every so often, training pauses and scores the validation set. While
both losses fall, the model is learning things that carry over to filings it hasn't seen. When the training loss keeps
falling but the validation loss turns up, the model has started to memorize its training filings. That is
**overfitting**. You keep the checkpoint where validation was best, and stopping there is called **early stopping**.

This job is a short version of that. It trains for 10 minutes, scores the validation filings once at the end, and you
use those scores, named `valid/...` on the board, to choose between settings. The test filings are I1's 1,000, so that
every run can also be compared with Kev-4B as released on exactly the same filings. Choose by validation even though
the test scores, `test/...`, sit right beside them. If you tried ten settings and kept whichever did best on the test
filings, part of its score would be luck, and its test score would overstate how well it does on new filings.

### Tips for training a language model

- **Change one thing at a time.** Keep the seed and every other setting the same, so that any difference comes from
  the one you changed. The job fixes the seed (`--seed 0`), so runs on the same GPU see the same filings in the same
  order.
- **Size the run to the time you have.** Time a short run first, then choose the number of steps that fits your slot.
  The learning rate's schedule needs that number before training starts.
- **Get the learning rate right.** Too small and the model barely moves. Too large and the updates overshoot and undo
  what the model already knew. A new LoRA adapter usually wants a larger learning rate, around 1e-4 to 2e-4, than
  continuing a trained adapter or training every weight, around 1e-5 to 5e-5.
- **Change the batch size and the learning rate together.** A bigger batch gives smoother but fewer updates from the
  same filings, and a somewhat larger learning rate often makes up for the fewer steps.
- **Read the loss's trend, not one line.** Each step sees different filings, so the loss jumps around. Over the run it
  should fall and then flatten. A loss that climbs, or turns into `nan`, usually means the learning rate is too high.
- **Judge on validation, not on the training loss.** A training loss that keeps falling while the validation scores
  get worse means the model is memorizing its training filings, which is called overfitting.
  [Training and validation](#training-and-validation) shows what that looks like.
- **Save checkpoints on long runs,** so that a job stopped by its time limit, or by a node that fails, can continue
  from the last one instead of starting over.
- **Fit the memory first.** If training runs out of GPU memory, the usual fixes are fewer filings on the GPU at once
  with more accumulation, gradient checkpointing, a bf16 copy of the frozen weights, or LoRA instead of training every
  weight.

<summary>What uses GPU memory when training</summary>

Training needs everything inference needs, plus the gradients and the intermediate results for the backward pass. We
measured it on an A40 (the peak memory PyTorch allocated, 64 steps of 8 filings):

| | fp32 backbone | bf16 backbone |
|---|---|---|
| Frozen weights | 16.8 GB | 8.4 GB |
| Adapter, head, gradients and AdamW | 0.5 GB | 0.5 GB |
| The rest: intermediate results and temporary bf16 copies | 5.0 GB | 3.7 GB |
| **Peak on the GPU** | **22.3 GB** | **12.6 GB** |
| Peak in the node's memory | 26.4 GB | 9.8 GB |
| Minutes to train on 512 filings | 9.2 | 8.6 |

Both fit in an A40's 48 GB with room to spare. The fp32 backbone also needs 26 GB of the node's own memory while it
loads, which is why the job asks for 32 GB.

## Run it: fine-tune for 10 minutes

By default the job **continues training Kev-4B's adapter**. The Qwen 4B backbone stays frozen, and Kev-4B's trained
LoRA adapter and answer head train further on Jev's labels. One Slurm job does all of it.

1. Slurm gives the job one GPU on a Yen GPU node. It is an A40 unless you ask for an H200.
2. The job loads Qwen 4B, frozen, and Kev-4B's adapter and answer head, the parts it trains.
3. It trains for about 10 minutes. On an A40 that is 512 filings from `train.jsonl`, 8 per step, so 64 steps.
4. It serves the new adapter, labels the 2,000 validation filings and I1's 1,000 test filings, and scores both sets
   against Jev's answers.
5. It sends the loss, the settings and the scores to W&B, which is on the board.

{: .important }
> You have time for **one** training job, so choose your settings before you submit it. Every participant's run goes
> to the same W&B project on the board, so you can see what the other settings did from everyone else's runs.

<details markdown="1" id="what-each-setting-does">
<summary>What each setting does, and what it trades</summary>

The [exercises](#compare-settings) show what each one did when we ran it.

#### Start from

By default the job continues Kev-4B's adapter, so it starts from everything Kev-4B already learned, and the 10
minutes add to it. With `INIT=scratch`, it trains a new adapter on plain Qwen 4B instead. That starts from nothing,
so it shows what 10 minutes of Jev's labels can do on their own, and it is the only way to choose the rank. A new
adapter's learning rate defaults to 2e-4, ten times higher, because it has further to go.

#### Rank

The rank sets how many numbers the adapter has to learn with. A rank-4 adapter has 8.1 million, rank 16 has 32.5
million and rank 64 has 130 million. On an A40, 10 minutes of training is only 512 filings, which may be too few to
put a bigger adapter to use. Rank needs `INIT=scratch`, because Kev-4B's adapter is rank 16 and the job won't
continue it at another rank. You can choose 4, 16 or 64.

#### Learning rate

The learning rate sets how far each update moves the trained numbers. If it is too small, the adapter barely moves
from where it started in 64 steps. If it is too large, the updates can overshoot and undo some of what the model
already knew. Any value from 2e-6 to 2e-4 is worth trying. 2e-5 is a short way of writing 2 × 10⁻⁵, or 0.00002.

#### Batch size (records per step)

This is how many filings the job adds up before it makes one update. The job trains on the same filings in about the
same time whatever you choose, so this sets how many updates you get. On an A40 that is 256 updates at 2 per step, 64
at 8 and 16 at 32. Fewer filings per update give more updates, each one noisier. More filings per update give
smoother updates, but fewer of them. A bigger batch doesn't need more GPU memory here, because the job still works on
one filing at a time and adds up their gradients. The exercises compare 2, 8 and 32.

#### Frozen backbone

The backbone never changes, but you can choose how its numbers are stored. fp32 is how Kev-4B was trained, and takes
16.8 GB. bf16 takes half that, 8.4 GB, and trains a little faster. The risk is that rounding the backbone's numbers
changes the answers. [What uses GPU memory when training](#what-uses-gpu-memory-when-training) has the measurements.
You can choose fp32 or bf16.

#### GPU

The H200 trains about 3.5 times as fast as the A40, so in the same 10 minutes it gets through 1,920 filings instead
of 512. The Yens have only two H200s, shared by all the participants, so an H200 job may wait in the queue before it
starts. You can choose the A40 or the H200.

</details>

### Comparing runs

The 2,000 validation filings are a sample, and a different sample would move every score a little. Every run is
scored on the same filings. Between two runs, a difference smaller than about 0.005 in weighted agreement, or 0.03 in
agreement on CEO changes, could come from which filings happen to be in the sample. That is only the sample's noise.
Training on different filings would move the scores too.

If you run a second job of your own, you can score your two runs side by side:

```bash
$PY scripts/intermediate/score.py $WORK/results/i2/<jobid>-valid.jsonl $WORK/results/i2/<other jobid>-valid.jsonl --sample $WORK/data/valid.parquet
```
{: .yens }

Kev-4B as released, on the validation filings, is the baseline every run is compared with:

| Kev-4B as released, 2,000 validation filings | |
|---|---|
| Agreement, weighted | 0.882 |
| Agreement on CEO changes | 0.442 |
| CEO-change F1 | 0.743 |
| "Leaves entirely" | 21.0% |

To score it yourself, on both sets: `RUN=jaredpalmer/kev-4b@v1.0 sbatch slurm/yens/intermediate/finetune_kev.slurm`.

{: .warning }
> Compare runs on the same set of filings only. "Agreement on CEO changes" counts every CEO change in the sample
> equally, and the two samples hold different mixes. Half of the validation CEO changes are "leaves entirely", against
> 29% of the test ones. So Kev-4B as released agrees on 0.442 of the validation CEO changes but 0.587 of the test ones,
> though it is the same model. Weighted agreement stands for the whole split in both cases, 0.882 and 0.884.

To score a run on the test filings again, without training it again:

```bash
EVAL=test RUN=$WORK/runs/i2/<jobid> sbatch slurm/yens/intermediate/finetune_kev.slurm
```
{: .yens }

`RUN` scores a run without training it. For a quicker training job, `EVAL=valid` skips the test filings.

<details markdown="1" id="why-only-10-minutes">
<summary>Why only 10 minutes, and where the job's time goes</summary>

On your own laptop you can let training run until it finishes. On a shared cluster you usually can't. A Slurm job has
a time limit (`#SBATCH -t`), and Slurm stops the job when it runs out. A reservation, like the one set aside for this
hackathon, ends at a set time. Your project has only so many GPU-hours. So you don't train until done: you find out how
fast a step is, then plan a run that fits the slot you have. This exercise gives each run a 10-minute slot.

Each run gets about 10 minutes of training, so it can use only a small slice of the training data. That is 512 of
the 52,757 filings in `train.jsonl` on an A40 (1%), or 1,920 on an H200 (3.6%).

| On one GPU | Filings | Share of `train.jsonl` | Training time |
|---|---|---|---|
| One run of this exercise, A40 | 512 | 1% | 9.2 minutes |
| One run of this exercise, H200 | 1,920 | 3.6% | 9.7 minutes |
| One full pass, A40 | 52,757 | 100% | 15.0 hours (13.7 with the bf16 backbone) |
| One full pass, H200 | 52,757 | 100% | 4.4 hours (4.2 with the bf16 backbone) |

The job stops after a set number of filings, not after a set time. The learning rate's schedule needs the number of
steps before training starts, so `FILINGS` is set to what fits in about 10 minutes on the GPU the job lands on. That
is 512 filings at 1.08 seconds each on an A40, and 1,920 at 0.31 seconds each on an H200. The job's 45-minute limit
allows up to about 1,800 filings on an A40 and 7,000 on an H200. Runs on the same GPU see the same filings, chosen by
`--seed 0`. The full-pass time is the `epoch hours` in your log.

The whole job takes about 19 minutes on an A40, because training is not all it does:

| Part of the job | A40 |
|---|---|
| Load the model and the filings | 2 to 3 minutes |
| Train on 512 filings, 64 steps of 8 | 9 minutes |
| Serve the new adapter, then label and score the 2,000 validation and 1,000 test filings | about 7 minutes |

On an H200 the job trains on 1,920 filings in the same 10 minutes and labels about 3.7 times as fast.

So the scores in this exercise show what a few percent of the data can do. On an A40, weighted agreement on
validation went from 0.882 to 0.961. The H200's extra filings, in the same 10 minutes, mostly helped on CEO changes.
With the default settings, agreement on them rose from 0.744 to 0.840 on validation, while weighted agreement barely
moved (0.961 to 0.962). Neither shows how much a full pass would add. On Marlowe, with many more GPUs, a full pass
becomes practical.

</details>

### Compare settings

Each question changes one setting from the default. Look for those runs on the board first, then open our answers,
which come from one run of each setting on an A40.

<details class="quiz" markdown="1">
<summary><span class="qnum">2</span><span class="qtext">Does storing the frozen backbone in bf16 instead of fp32 change memory, speed or the answers?</span></summary>

```bash
WEIGHTS=bf16 sbatch slurm/yens/intermediate/finetune_kev.slurm
```
{: .yens }

| | fp32 backbone (default) | bf16 backbone |
|---|---|---|
| Peak GPU memory | 22.3 GB | 12.6 GB |
| Peak node memory | 26.4 GB | 9.8 GB |
| Training time | 9.2 min | 8.6 min |
| Agreement, weighted | 0.961 | 0.961 |
| Agreement on CEO changes | 0.744 | 0.740 |

It saves memory and a little time, and barely changes the answers. The two adapters gave the same status on 1,991 of
the 2,000 filings. With mixed precision, the matrix multiplications run in bf16 either way. An fp32 backbone is
rounded to a temporary bf16 copy for each one, so storing it in bf16 only skips those copies. The numbers being
trained, `A`, `B` and the head, stay in fp32 in both runs.

</details>

<details class="quiz" markdown="1">
<summary><span class="qnum">3</span><span class="qtext">What happens with a learning rate ten times smaller (2e-6) or ten times larger (2e-4)?</span></summary>

```bash
LR=2e-6 sbatch slurm/yens/intermediate/finetune_kev.slurm
LR=2e-4 sbatch slurm/yens/intermediate/finetune_kev.slurm
```
{: .yens }

| Learning rate | Agreement, weighted | On CEO changes | Reason | "Temporary leave" | Loss at step 60 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2e-6 | 0.938 | 0.636 | 0.890 | 78.6% | 0.304 |
| 2e-5 (default) | 0.961 | 0.744 | 0.923 | 78.6% | 0.242 |
| 2e-4 | 0.962 | 0.706 | 0.888 | 57.1% | 0.212 |

At 2e-6 the steps are too small. In 64 steps the adapter moves only part of the way from Kev-4B as released (0.882
and 0.442). At 2e-4 the weighted agreement matches the default, but agreement on CEO changes and their reasons falls.
The steps are big enough to undo some of what Kev-4B knew, and "temporary leave" fell from 11 of its 14 filings to 8.
The training loss can't tell 2e-5 and 2e-4 apart, since it moves more than that from one line to the next, but the
validation scores can.

</details>

<details class="quiz" markdown="1">
<summary><span class="qnum">4</span><span class="qtext">Train on the same 512 filings with 2 or with 32 records per step. Which does better, and which is faster?</span></summary>

```bash
RECORDS_PER_STEP=2 sbatch slurm/yens/intermediate/finetune_kev.slurm
RECORDS_PER_STEP=32 sbatch slurm/yens/intermediate/finetune_kev.slurm
```
{: .yens }

| Records per step | Steps | Agreement, weighted | On CEO changes | CEO-change F1 | Reason | Training time |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 256 | 0.961 | 0.802 | 0.946 | 0.947 | 9.3 min |
| 8 (default) | 64 | 0.961 | 0.744 | 0.912 | 0.923 | 9.2 min |
| 32 | 16 | 0.956 | 0.739 | 0.919 | 0.909 | 9.2 min |

Neither is faster. Every filing goes forward and backward through the model once whatever the setting, so the GPU
does the same work. What changes is the number of updates. Two per step was the best run we measured. It had 0.058
more agreement on CEO changes than the default, well beyond chance, and it was the only run that did at least as well
as Kev-4B as released on every status. AdamW moves each number by up to about the learning rate at every update,
however many filings went into it, so 256 updates moved the adapter further than 64 or 16. With 32 per step, the
model picks "leaves entirely" more often than Jev does, 536 times against Jev's 509, and "interim steps aside" fell to
47.9%.

</details>

<details class="quiz" markdown="1">
<summary><span class="qnum">5</span><span class="qtext">Train a new adapter from the base model at ranks 4, 16 and 64. How does it compare with continuing Kev-4B's?</span></summary>

```bash

INIT=scratch RANK=16 sbatch slurm/yens/intermediate/finetune_kev.slurm
INIT=scratch RANK=64 sbatch slurm/yens/intermediate/finetune_kev.slurm
```
{: .yens }

| Start from | Rank | Agreement, weighted | On CEO changes | Reason | Peak GPU memory |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kev-4B as released, no training | 16 | 0.882 | 0.442 | 0.869 | |
| Continue Kev-4B (default) | 16 | 0.961 | 0.744 | 0.923 | 22.3 GB |
| Scratch, learning rate 2e-4 | 4 | 0.946 | 0.655 | 0.920 | 21.9 GB |
| Scratch, learning rate 2e-4 | 16 | 0.953 | 0.642 | 0.897 | 22.3 GB |
| Scratch, learning rate 2e-4 | 64 | 0.950 | 0.626 | 0.729 | 23.9 GB |

A new adapter and a new answer head, trained on 512 filings, already beat Kev-4B as released at every rank. But
continuing Kev-4B's adapter did better still. What Kev-4B had learned before is worth about 0.1 in agreement on CEO
changes here. The rank made no clear difference to agreement, since 0.655, 0.642 and 0.626 are within chance of each
other. With only 512 filings, more numbers to learn don't help. Rank 64 agreed with Jev's reasons less often and
missed all 14 temporary leaves. Memory hardly grows with rank, because even rank 64 is small next to the backbone.

</details>

### Your chosen run on the test filings
