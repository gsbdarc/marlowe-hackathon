# B5. From labels to a finding

**Objective:** turn the labels you made in B4 into a research result about CEO turnover, and find out how far you can
trust a result built on a model's labels. First you explore with a ready-made script and choose a question; then you
write your own analysis in Python and leave with a chart.
{: .fs-6 .fw-300 }

| | |
|---|---|
| Where | Marlowe's login node: no GPU, no job to submit |
| Data | your B4 labels, and Jev's labels for the same filings |
| Model | none: this is the part of the work that comes after the GPUs |
| Time | about 1 hour 30 minutes: 30 minutes exploring, then an hour writing your own analysis |

## What you have

Your two B4 jobs labeled between 2 and 8 of the 8 piles: 74,000 to 296,477 filings. Because the filings were dealt
into piles in filing order, each pile is a spread across every year, like a systematic sample. With fewer than 8
piles your **shares** (CEO changes per 100 filings, the share forced out) are estimates from a sample, and your
**counts** are for your sample only. The script says which you have.

You also have a check that most research with model labels does not: **Jev labeled every one of these filings too.**
So for every number Kev gives you, you can see what Jev says. Keep in mind that Jev is a model as well, a bigger and
more expensive one: when the two disagree, the filing itself is the only judge.

<details class="quiz" markdown="1">
<summary><span class="qnum">1</span><span class="qtext">Using Kev's column alone, did CEO turnover rise or fall from 2004 to 2019? Now with Jev's?</span></summary>

By Kev, it **rose**: from about 11 CEO changes per 100 filings in 2004 to 2006 to about 13 in 2019. By Jev, it
**fell**: from about 15 to about 13. Same filings, opposite conclusions. The difference is not in the companies but in
the labeler: Kev-4B as released misses many of the CEO changes in the early years, and much fewer later on.

</details>

<details class="quiz" markdown="1">
<summary><span class="qnum">2</span><span class="qtext">Kev agrees with Jev on 88% of filings. Why is that not enough to trust Kev's trend?</span></summary>

Because the errors are not spread evenly. If Kev missed the same share of CEO changes every year, its counts would be
too low, but the trend would be right. Its misses are concentrated in the early years, so they bend the trend itself.
An overall accuracy number cannot tell you that: you have to check the errors against the thing you are measuring,
here time. In econometrics terms, the measurement error is correlated with the variable you care about.

</details>

## Your decision: pick your question

Choose one of three. Spread them around your table so the room covers all three, and be ready to report what you
found.

### A. Is the trend real?

Look again at the 2004 to 2006 rows. Then read some of the filings where Kev and Jev disagree, in a year of your
choice, and decide for yourself who is right:

```bash
$PY scripts/beginner/findings.py read $WORK/results/b4/$ONE $WORK/results/b4/$MINE --year 2005 --disagree
```
{: .marlowe }

Each filing shows Kev's answer and how sure it was, Jev's answer, and the filing's text. Change `--year`, and add
`--seed 1`, `--seed 2` and so on for other filings. Read at least five, and keep a tally: Kev right, Jev right, or
hard to say.

### B. Why do CEOs leave?

Each filing also answers *why* the CEO left. Compare the reasons across periods, including the 2008 to 2009 crisis and
the pandemic:

```bash
$PY scripts/beginner/findings.py reasons $WORK/results/b4/$ONE $WORK/results/b4/$MINE
```
{: .marlowe }

It prints the reasons by period from Kev, then from Jev, then the share of CEOs forced out (pushed out over
performance, or dismissed over legal or policy violations) each year. Are more CEOs forced out in the crisis years?
Do Kev and Jev tell the same story? What does "no reason" mean for a study of *why* CEOs leave?

### C. Read the filings

Pick a year and a reason, and read what Kev found:

```bash
$PY scripts/beginner/findings.py read $WORK/results/b4/$ONE $WORK/results/b4/$MINE --year 2009 --reason retired
```
{: .marlowe }

The reasons are `died`, `illness`, `performance`, `legal/policy`, `retired`, `new job`, `other` and `no reason`.
`--examples 5` shows five filings instead of three, and `--seed` picks others. For each one: do you agree with Kev?
Was Kev sure? Would you have coded it the same way?

## What the room found

When the room has posted, read the B5 thread together, and answer these from everyone's findings.

<details class="quiz" markdown="1">
<summary><span class="qnum">3</span><span class="qtext">Look at how sure Kev was in the filings where it disagreed with Jev. What do you notice?</span></summary>

Kev was usually unsure: when it said "not a CEO change" and Jev said the CEO left, its top answer often had only 20%
to 30% confidence, barely ahead of the other seven options. Low confidence is where a model's labels are weakest.
That gives you a cheap check on any labeled data set: read a sample of the least confident labels, by hand, before you
trust the rest.

</details>

<details class="quiz" markdown="1">
<summary><span class="qnum">4</span><span class="qtext">People who labeled 2 piles and people who labeled 8 got nearly the same per-100 numbers. Why?</span></summary>

Because a share does not need every filing, only a fair sample of them. Two piles are a quarter of the filings,
spread evenly across the years, so the share in them is close to the share in all. What does change with the sample
is the noise: a year with few filings, like 2004, moves more between people with small samples. It is the same
reasoning as any survey, and a reason to label a sample first and look before you label everything.

</details>

<details class="quiz" markdown="1">
<summary><span class="qnum">5</span><span class="qtext">You want to publish a finding about CEO turnover from labels like these. What would you do differently?</span></summary>

There is more than one good answer. Some that came up today:

- **Use a better labeler**, or a trained one: B2 lifted agreement on CEO changes from 0.59 to about 0.79 in 10
  minutes, and the early-year gap would likely shrink.
- **Check the errors against what you measure**: compare the model with a trusted labeler by year, as Step 1 did, not
  only overall.
- **Read a sample by hand**, especially the least confident labels, and report how often the model was right.
- **Report how the labels were made**, so a reader can judge them: the model, its version, and how well it agreed with
  a reference.

</details>

## Your own analysis

So far a script did the counting for you. Now you write it. `scripts/beginner/my_analysis.py` loads your labels into
a table, one row per filing, and leaves five exercises for you to fill in, each marked `TODO`. The loading is done;
each exercise is one to five lines of Python with [pandas](https://pandas.pydata.org/docs/user_guide/10min.html).
Every exercise has a hint, and a worked answer you can open if you are stuck: opening it is fine, but try first.

Run the file once as it is. It loads the labels and says that each exercise is not done yet:

```bash
$PY scripts/beginner/my_analysis.py $WORK/results/b4/$ONE $WORK/results/b4/$MINE
```
{: .marlowe }

To edit it, open it in `nano`, a simple text editor that runs in the terminal:

```bash

```

of the file first: it lists the columns you have.

Each time it runs, the script also puts your progress on [the board](https://forge.coreweave.com/wandb/Marlowe-Hackathon),
in the **beginner-5-analysis** project, as one run named with your SUNet ID: how many exercises are done, your

### Exercise 1: CEO changes by year

Fill in `per_year`: Kev's CEO changes per 100 filings, by year. When it is right, it matches the Kev column of
`findings.py trend` from Step 1.

<details class="quiz" markdown="1">
<summary><span class="qnum">E1</span><span class="qtext">Hint, then the answer</span></summary>

`d.groupby("year")` groups the filings by year. `kev_change` is True or False for each filing, and the mean of True
and False values is the share that are True. Multiply by 100 for "per 100 filings":

```python
per_year = d.groupby("year").kev_change.mean() * 100
```

</details>

### Exercise 2: can confidence fix the trend?

In Step 1 Kev undercounted CEO changes in the early years, and in [What the room found](#what-the-room-found) its
mistakes were the labels it was unsure of. So here is an idea: keep only the labels Kev was sure of. Fill in `sure`,
the filings with `kev_status_conf` of at least `MIN_CONF`, and `compare`, a table by year with Kev's and Jev's CEO
changes per 100 of those filings, and the share of each year's filings you kept. Run it at `MIN_CONF = 0.5`, then 0.8.

<details class="quiz" markdown="1">
<summary><span class="qnum">E2</span><span class="qtext">Hint, then the answer</span></summary>

`d[d.kev_status_conf >= MIN_CONF]` keeps the rows where the condition is True. `pd.DataFrame({...})` puts several
series with the same index (here, the year) side by side as columns:

```python
sure = d[d.kev_status_conf >= MIN_CONF]
compare = pd.DataFrame({
    "kev": sure.groupby("year").kev_change.mean() * 100,
    "jev": sure.groupby("year").jev_change.mean() * 100,
    "kept": sure.groupby("year").size() / d.groupby("year").size() * 100,
})
```

</details>

<details class="quiz" markdown="1">
<summary><span class="qnum">6</span><span class="qtext">At 50%, Kev and Jev now agree almost exactly. Did confidence fix the trend?</span></summary>

No, it broke something else. Look at the levels and at `kept`: on the filings Kev was sure of, there are only about
4 CEO changes per 100 filings, against about 12 in all of them, and you kept only about half the filings. The labels
Kev is unsure of are mostly the CEO changes, the hard cases, so dropping them drops the very thing you are counting.
Kev and Jev agree on what is left because what is left is easy. Filtering on confidence selects the sample on the
outcome. It is a fine way to choose which labels to check by hand, and a poor way to choose which to keep.

</details>

<details class="quiz" markdown="1">
<summary><span class="qnum">7</span><span class="qtext">Jev's line drops sharply from 2006 to 2007, and Kev's does not. What could cause a jump like that in a series built from filings?</span></summary>

A change in what gets filed, not in how often CEOs leave. When the rules for a filing change, the mix of filings
changes with them. The SEC changed what companies must report under Item 5.02 in late 2006, adding more filings about
pay and other officers, so from 2007 there are many more filings that are not about the CEO, and the share that are
CEO changes falls. Whenever a series is "per 100 filings", check whether the denominator changed. And note that the
two labelers do not even agree on the size of the jump.

</details>

### Exercise 4: your own question

Pick one of the four questions in the file and write the code for its table in `mine`. To draw it, the file has a
helper, `chart()`: take the `#` off the `chart(...)` line under Exercise 4 and change its title and label. It draws
every column of the table as a line, or bars with `kind="barh"`, and saves `b5-mine.png`. This one has hints, but no
single answer:

- **a. Months.** Do CEO changes cluster in some months? Group by `month`; per 100 filings is fairer than a count, since
  some months have more filings of every kind.
- **b. Companies.** Which companies changed CEO most often? Group the CEO changes by `cik` and `company_name`, count
  them, sort, and keep the top 10 with `.head(10)`. Then read the top company's filings: `findings.py read` with
  `--cik` and its number, and `--examples 10`. Are they all real?
- **c. Announced ahead.** Are CEO changes announced ahead of time more often now than in 2005? On the CEO changes,
  average `kev_future` by year, and Jev's `p_future` next to it.
- **d. Length.** Are forced departures described in longer filings? Compare the median `words` of CEO changes with
  `kev_forced` True and False.

<details class="quiz" markdown="1">
<summary><span class="qnum">E4</span><span class="qtext">A worked answer for question c, and what we found for the others</span></summary>

```python
changes = d[d.kev_change]
mine = pd.DataFrame({
    "kev": changes.groupby("year").kev_future.mean(),
    "jev": d[d.jev_change].groupby("year").p_future.mean(),
})
```

and, on the `chart(...)` line:

```python
chart(mine, "CEO changes announced ahead of time", "share announced ahead", "b5-mine.png")
```

What our labels showed:

- **a.** May and June have the most CEO changes per filing, about 14 per 100 against 10 to 12 in other months: around
  companies' annual meetings.
- **b.** The top company has 20 "CEO changes" by Kev in 22 years, but 16 by Jev, and the two agree on only 9 of them.
  For the next two companies Kev counts 19 and 18, Jev 12 and 9. A ranking of firms by turnover would come out
  different with each labeler: reading the filings is the only way to know which is right.
- **c.** By Jev, the share announced ahead of time rose from about 0.30 to about 0.44. By Kev, it barely moved, from
  0.34 to 0.39. One more trend that depends on the labeler.
- **d.** Slightly: a median of 340 words for forced departures against 313 for the rest.

</details>

### Exercise 5: a regression

Researchers do not stop at a chart. Fill in `kev_fit` and `jev_fit`: on the filings from 2004 to 2019, regress CEO
change (0 or 1, times 100) on the year, once with Kev's labels and once with Jev's. `linregress`, from the `scipy`
library, fits a straight line and returns its slope and the slope's standard error.

<details class="quiz" markdown="1">
<summary><span class="qnum">E5</span><span class="qtext">Hint, then the answer</span></summary>

`d.year.between(2004, 2019)` is True for the years you want. `.astype(float)` turns True and False into 1 and 0:

```python
p = d[d.year.between(2004, 2019)]
kev_fit = linregress(p.year, p.kev_change.astype(float) * 100)
jev_fit = linregress(p.year, p.jev_change.astype(float) * 100)
```

```
Kev: slope +0.137 a year, standard error 0.016, t = 8.4
Jev: slope -0.086 a year, standard error 0.017, t = -5.0
```
{: .output }

</details>

<details class="quiz" markdown="1">
<summary><span class="qnum">8</span><span class="qtext">Both slopes are far from zero, with t-statistics of 8 and 5. Which result is right, and what does the standard error tell you?</span></summary>

They cannot both be right: one says CEO turnover rose, the other that it fell. The standard errors are small because
there are over 200,000 filings, and they measure only sampling noise: how much the slope would move with a different
sample of filings labeled **the same way**. They say nothing about whether the labels are right. Kev's errors are
concentrated in the early years, so they are correlated with the regressor, and that biases the slope, here enough to
flip its sign. More data makes a biased estimate more precise, not less biased. Before you trust a coefficient built on
model labels, check the labels against the regressor, as Step 1 did.

</details>

<details class="quiz" markdown="1">
<summary><span class="qnum">9</span><span class="qtext">What did our trained model show?</span></summary>
