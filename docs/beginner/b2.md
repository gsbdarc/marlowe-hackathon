# B2. Fine-tune the model

**Objective:** take the same Kev-4B from B1 and *train* it for about 10 minutes on Jev's answers, then see how far its
score moves. You make one real decision, the learning rate, and the room makes it the way a researcher should: on
validation filings, before anyone looks at the test score.
{: .fs-6 .fw-300 }

| | |
|---|---|
| Where | Yens, 1 GPU |
| Data | thousands of filings with Jev's answers, to train on; 2,000 validation filings, to choose on; the same 1,000 test filings as B1, to report |
| Model | Kev-4B, trained a little further, against Kev-4B as released from B1 |
| Time | about 50 minutes: a 22-minute job, reading while it runs, and comparing with the room |

---

You need B1 done: a score for Kev-4B as released, and you know how to submit a job and read its log. Keep your B1

```bash
cd /scratch/users/$USER/marlowe-hackathon
source scripts/beginner/yens.sh
```

## What "training" means here

In B1, Kev-4B answered the filings exactly as it was released. **Training** (also called **fine-tuning**) changes the
model's numbers a little so its answers line up better with Jev's. It works like studying with an answer key:

1. The model reads a filing and gives its answer, as a probability for each option.
2. We compare those probabilities with Jev's and measure how far off they are. This gap is called the **loss**.
   Jev's answer is its whole spread of probabilities, not only its top choice, so when Jev was torn between two
   options, the model learns that too. Training a small model on a big one's answers is called **distillation**.
3. The model nudges its numbers to shrink the loss.
4. After 8 filings, it takes one **step**: it applies the nudges from those 8 together. Then it reads the next 8.

Do this on enough filings and the model gets better at the task. You will watch the loss fall as it trains, and then
see the score rise when you test the trained model. [Key Ideas]({{ '/concepts/' | relative_url }}) has these terms,
and **epoch**, on one page.

{: .note }
> **It trains only a small part.** The model has about 4 billion numbers, and training all of them needs a lot of GPU
> memory. Instead, this job freezes the big model and trains a small add-on (about 1% of its size) that sits on top.
> That takes far less memory, which is why 10 minutes on one GPU is enough to see a real gain. If you want the full
> story, the Intermediate track's [I2]({{ '/intermediate/i2/' | relative_url }}) explains it (the add-on is called
> LoRA).

<details class="quiz" markdown="1">
<summary><span class="qnum">1</span><span class="qtext">Before you run it: the model trains on filings from 2004 to 2018, and you score it on filings from 2022 to 2026. Why not score it on the filings it trained on?</span></summary>

Because it has seen their answers. A model can memorize the answer key, and scoring it on the same filings would
measure memory, not skill. Scoring on filings it never saw, from later years, measures whether it learned something
that carries over to new documents, which is the only thing you care about when you use it for real.

</details>

## Three sets of filings

The job uses three separate sets of filings, and keeping them apart is the most important idea on this page:

| Set | Filings | Years | What it is for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Training** | 512 of over 50,000 | 2004 to 2018 | the model learns from Jev's answers on these |
| **Validation** | 2,000 | 2019 to 2021 | you **choose** your settings by the score on these |
| **Test** | 1,000, the same as B1 | 2022 to 2026 | you **report** the score on these, once, after choosing |

Why not choose on the test filings directly? If you try several settings and keep the one with the best test score,
part of what you kept is luck: the setting that happened to suit those particular 1,000 filings. The test score then
overstates how well the model will do on new filings. Choosing on validation and scoring the winner on test keeps the
test score honest, because it played no part in the choice.

<details class="quiz" markdown="1">
<summary><span class="qnum">2</span><span class="qtext">What is the same mistake in an empirical paper?</span></summary>

Specification search: trying many regressions and reporting the one with the best fit or the smallest p-value. The
reported result looks better than the method deserves, for the same reason, and the remedies are the same too: hold
out data you do not look at while choosing, or fix the choice before you see the outcome, as a pre-registration does.

</details>

## Your decision: the learning rate

Training nudges the model's numbers a little at every step. The **learning rate** sets how big each nudge is. It is
the setting that most often decides whether a fine-tune works, and there is no formula for it: people try a few
values and compare, which is what the room does today. You choose one of three:

| Learning rate | How to submit | What could happen |
|---|---|---|
| 2e-6 (0.000002) | `LR=2e-6` before `sbatch` | small, careful nudges: safe, but in 64 steps the model may barely move |
| 2e-5 (the default) | no setting | ten times larger |
| 2e-4 | `LR=2e-4` before `sbatch` | big nudges: learns fastest, but may overshoot and undo some of what Kev-4B already knew |

Spread the choices around your table, so the room has runs of all three. **Write down your prediction before you
submit:** which learning rate will score highest on validation, and why?

{: .note }
> If you would rather not choose, run the default. You still get every result on this page, and you can compare with
> the room's runs of the other two.

## Run it

One command does everything: it trains for about 10 minutes, then starts the trained model, scores it on the 2,000
validation filings, and then on the 1,000 test filings. Run **one** of these. The default:

```bash
sbatch --reservation=hackathon slurm/yens/beginner/finetune.slurm
```
{: .yens }

Or put your learning rate in front. `LR=2e-6` before `sbatch` hands that setting to the job, which reads it instead of
the default:

```bash
LR=2e-6 sbatch --reservation=hackathon slurm/yens/beginner/finetune.slurm
```
{: .yens }

```bash
LR=2e-4 sbatch --reservation=hackathon slurm/yens/beginner/finetune.slurm
```
{: .yens }

Type the job ID it prints into the box above the next command, and follow the log the same way as in B1:

```bash
tail -f logs/ceo-finetune-<jobid>.out
```
{: .yens }

{: .important }
> The whole job takes about 22 minutes: roughly 10 training, the rest loading the model and scoring. You do not need
> to wait at the screen. Ctrl-C stops `tail`, not the job; `squeue --me` shows whether it is still running.

Every ten steps the log prints a line like this:

```
ep0 step 40/64 loss 0.319 kl 0.000 anchor 0.000 1.033s/rec
```
{: .output }

| Part | What it means |
|---|---|
| `ep0` | the first pass through the training filings (an **epoch** is one full pass; you will do a small part of one) |
| `step 40/64` | step 40 of the 64 this job takes: 64 steps of 8 filings is 512 filings |
| `loss 0.319` | how far the answers are from Jev's, averaged over the last 10 steps: watch it fall |
| `1.033s/rec` | about how many seconds each filing ("record") takes, averaged so far |

After training, the job prints a timing line, starts the trained model, and scores it twice: a line `== valid`, then
a score table for validation, then `== test` and a score table for test.

## Watch the loss on the board

This job is on the screen too, in the **beginner-2-finetune** project on
[the board](https://forge.coreweave.com/wandb/Marlowe-Hackathon). Each run's name includes its learning rate
(`lr2e-5`). Open yours and watch the **loss** chart draw itself as the job trains: a line sloping down, with some
wobble. A falling loss means the model is learning to answer more like Jev.

<details class="quiz" markdown="1">
<summary><span class="qnum">3</span><span class="qtext">The loss line wobbles up and down instead of falling smoothly. Is something wrong?</span></summary>

No. Each step learns from only 8 filings, and some batches of 8 are harder than others, so the loss on any one step
jumps around. What matters is the trend over many steps. That is why the board can smooth the line for you.

</details>

<details class="quiz" markdown="1">
<summary><span class="qnum">4</span><span class="qtext">Compare the loss lines of the three learning rates on the board. Can you choose the best one from the loss?</span></summary>

Not really. The loss measures the training filings, the ones the model is learning from, and it wobbles more from one
step to the next than the lines differ. And a model that fits its training filings more closely is not always better
on new ones: in our runs, 2e-4 usually ended with the lowest loss (about 0.22, against 0.24 at 2e-5), yet it usually
scored lower on validation. What you care about is new filings, which is what
validation measures.

</details>

## The room decides, on validation

When your job finishes, print just its validation scores, the line for `<jobid>-valid.jsonl` and the header above

{: .yens }

Then collect the room's: the board shows each run's `valid/` scores next to its learning rate, and the 🧵 **B2**
thread has everyone's. For each learning rate, the validation agreement on CEO changes:

| Learning rate | Validation: agreement on CEO changes | Validation: all filings (weighted) |
|---|---|---|
| 2e-6 | | |
| 2e-5 | | |
| 2e-4 | | |

Copy this into your notes and fill it in from the room's runs. Several people ran each learning rate, so you can see
how much the same setting varies from run to run, as well as how the settings differ. **Pick the room's winner from
this table alone**, before anyone looks at test. Was your prediction right?

<details class="quiz" markdown="1">
<summary><span class="qnum">5</span><span class="qtext">What did our runs show, and how sure can you be of the winner?</span></summary>

We ran each learning rate four times. Agreement on CEO changes, on the 2,000 validation filings:

| Learning rate | Run 1 | Run 2 | Run 3 | Run 4 | All filings (weighted), every run |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2e-6 | 0.633 | 0.637 | 0.634 | 0.633 | 0.94 |
| 2e-5 | 0.743 | 0.745 | 0.742 | 0.745 | 0.96 |
| 2e-4 | 0.764 | 0.655 | 0.711 | 0.669 | 0.96 |

2e-6 is clearly worse: its nudges are too small, and in 64 steps the model moves only part of the way from Kev-4B as
released. 2e-5 wins, and the repeats are why you can trust that: its four runs landed within 0.003 of each other.

2e-4 is the lesson. Its first run was the best of all twelve, and had we stopped there, we would have picked it. Its
next three scored 0.655 to 0.711. Training on a GPU is not exactly repeatable, and big nudges turn those tiny
differences into large ones, so the same setting lands somewhere different each time. Even the first run's lead was
within noise: the validation set has about 1,000 CEO changes, so each number is uncertain by about ±0.014 from the
sample alone. A setting that wins once and loses three times is not the winner, which is why the room runs each
learning rate several times.

</details>

## Before and after, on test

Now, and only now, look at your test scores, for the same 1,000 filings as B1:

```bash
grep -B1 -- '-test.jsonl ' logs/ceo-finetune-<jobid>.out
```
{: .yens }

```
               run  answered  status_agree_weighted  status_agree_on_ceo_changes  ceo_change_f1  ...
<jobid>-test.jsonl      1000                  0.964                         0.79          0.951  ...
```
{: .output }

That is our run at the default, 2e-5. Put the room's winner side by side with B1:

| 1,000 test filings | B1: Kev-4B as released | B2: after 10 minutes of training at the room's learning rate |
|---|---|---|
| Agreement, all filings (weighted) | your B1 number, about 0.88 | about 0.96 |
| Agreement on CEO changes | your B1 number, about 0.59 | about 0.79 |

The filings and the questions are identical, so the difference is entirely the training. Expect the CEO-change number
to move a lot more than the overall one: the easy filings were already right in B1, so the gain is almost all on the
hard ones.

<details class="quiz" markdown="1">
<summary><span class="qnum">6</span><span class="qtext">Does the learning rate that won on validation also win on test? What if it had not?</span></summary>

Yes, in our runs. On test, agreement on CEO changes was 0.68 to 0.69 at 2e-6, 0.79 to 0.80 at 2e-5, and 0.70 to
0.78 at 2e-4: the same order as validation, with 2e-4 again the most spread out. Even its lucky first run scored 0.776
on test, below every 2e-5 run.

Had the two disagreed, you would still report the test score of the validation winner. Switching to whichever setting
won on test would bring back exactly the luck the validation set was there to keep out, and in a paper it would be
reporting the best of several tries as if it were the only one. The same goes for runs: had one run per setting decided
the winner, 2e-4 would have won and you would report its 0.776, not 2e-5's 0.79.

</details>

<details class="quiz" markdown="1">
<summary><span class="qnum">7</span><span class="qtext">The model trained on 512 filings. There are over 50,000 training filings. Why stop so early?</span></summary>

Time. At about a second a filing on this GPU, all of them would take 15 hours. 512 filings is 10 minutes, and already
enough to move the score. The next two tasks are about getting more done in the same time: a faster GPU in B3, and
many GPUs at once in B4.

</details>

## Challenge: does twice the training help twice as much?

Make a prediction first, and write it down: if the model trains on 1,024 filings instead of 512, at the room's
learning rate, will the CEO-change number rise by twice as much as it did in B2?

Then test it. `FILINGS=1024` works like `LR`: the job reads it instead of its default of 512. Add `LR=` too if the
room's winner was not the default. The job takes about 28 minutes.

```bash
FILINGS=1024 sbatch --reservation=hackathon slurm/yens/beginner/finetune.slurm
```
{: .yens }

```bash
tail -f logs/ceo-finetune-<jobid>.out
```
{: .yens data-job="f1024" data-job-label="1,024-filing job ID" }

<details class="quiz" markdown="1">
<summary><span class="qnum">8</span><span class="qtext">Was your prediction right?</span></summary>

Usually not: the second 512 filings help less than the first. In our run at 2e-5, the first 512 lifted agreement on
CEO changes in the test filings from 0.59 to 0.79, and the next 512 only to 0.84: a quarter of the gain, for the same
training time. Early training fixes the model's biggest, most common mistakes; what is left are rarer, harder cases
that each step moves less. This is called **diminishing returns**, and it is why you measure before you buy hours of
GPU time: the score you get for the next hour is smaller than for the last one.

</details>
